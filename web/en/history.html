What changes when we change the calendar?

The date label changes, not the event itself. In countries that adopted the reform in 1582, Thursday 4 October in the Julian calendar was followed by Friday 15 October in the Gregorian calendar. Those people did not skip ten real days. Other countries switched later.

Traditional numbering has no year 0: 1 BCE is followed by 1 CE. Astronomical numbering calls that same previous year 0. This does not establish the year of Jesus’s birth.

We choose one calendar for the entire input. Gregorian or Julian rules are extended into the past and future. The application does not infer the local reform date, the historical start of the year or earlier Roman irregularities. For medieval records, check the source, country and dating convention; a date already converted should not be converted again as Julian.

Your calendar keeps its rules: the first month has 29 days in Gregorian leap years, the last always has 29, and the others have 28. We therefore convert through the same continuous day and Gregorian year, not just the day number within the source year.

Time is optional. We do not assume midnight when the time is unknown. When you enter it, we convert only the units within the same time basis; we do not calculate historical local zones, changes to day boundaries, sidereal time or Earth’s position.
